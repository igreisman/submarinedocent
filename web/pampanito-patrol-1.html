Pack was assigned Area D-2 in the Gulf of Siam / South China Sea. Pampanito served as pack commander throughout the patrol until turning command over to Caiman on 25 March 1945. Pack boats maintained an east-west staggered patrol line between Latitudes 5° and 6° N. from the Malay Coast eastward approximately 150 miles, with inshore boats submerging during daylight to reduce detection by enemy aircraft while seaward boats maintained surface cruising. It was during this plan that Sealion sank an unescorted tanker off the coast at night.

Aircraft Contacts

Thirty contacts with enemy planes were made while in the area. The pack was bothered throughout the patrol by efficient use of Japanese search planes. While no boat in the pack was able to confirm it, the evidence strongly suggests the Japanese kept track of all submarines and routed traffic accordingly. In only one instance was there evidence of airborne radar, on 23 March 1945. At no time in the area did the pack encounter planes at night. The vast majority of plane contacts were made by radar, leaving little information regarding aircraft types in operation.

On 21 March 1945, in position Lat. 05° 37' N. and Long. 104° 20' E., the wreckage of a Japanese plane was sighted in the water.

On one occasion while proceeding on the surface at full speed during daylight to intercept a contact — later identified as a hospital ship — the pack was forced down three different times by enemy planes which circled at about 8 miles. Just prior to diving on one of these occasions, a Japanese two-engine bomber escorted by a fighter was sighted circling at 7–8 miles. At this time, all five submarines including Pampanito were attempting to intercept the ship, and enemy surveillance was almost certain.

Major Defects & Damage
Hull & Machinery — Air Compressor Motor
#2 air compressor motor flooded out when a plug in the salt water circulating system carried away due to corrosion.
Hull & Machinery — Main Engine #1
Cracked cylinder liner water jacket — same engine on which three liners were replaced at last refit for the same defect. The crack occurred around the starting air adapter. Starting air adapters were found badly pitted or corroded, allowing exhaust gas to enter the water jacket, probably causing the cracks.
Hull & Machinery — Main Motors #2 & #4
Again developed a badly unbalanced condition after running with 75% or more load for a period of 24 hours or longer. Unbalance occurs instantaneously with #2 main motor taking the load. Load was transferred off motors before brushes burned and brush rigging on #2 motor shifted until the load balanced.
Hull & Machinery — Shunt Field Coils
Two shunt field coils and one interpole coil developed a zero ground and were replaced.
Navigation — Gyro Compass
Follow-up motor failed due to overheating. Motor was removed and spare installed. Bearing on the top of the vertical shaft of the follow-up gear train was found to be scored, causing the system to be very stiff. Since no spare bearing is carried it could not be replaced. The system was freed up as much as possible and the burned-up motor is being repaired.
Ordnance & Gunnery
No major defects or damages occurred in ordnance and gunnery equipment.
Radio

Two defects experienced: "C" frequency band in the RBH receiver, and No. 8 frequency band in one RLL filed. The signal from Bakers Fox faded daily between 0500 and 0700 zebra on all frequencies. A complete log of receiving conditions was submitted to CNO as a separate report. Pampanito cleared six messages to shore stations without too great delay — two direct to NPM and VIK0, and four via Fox/Fleet broadcast. A roger via Fox was received in all cases. Each boat would alert the pack when diving for planes by sending a spare code group until the antenna went under — this was very successful at all times.

Radar
SJ-1 Surface Search Radar
Operation was normal during this patrol. On 7 March 1945 two hours of operational time were lost when the "A" scope sweep disappeared — a shorted C-33.1 in the power supply for the cathode ray tube. On 19 March a thorough check was made but the trouble could not be found; P-11 (25,000 ohms) was replaced by spare P-8 (70,000 ohms). Reliable ranges: U.S. submarines 12,000 yds; Great Redang Island 80,000 yds; U.S. Destroyer Escort 21,000 yds; Unknown Japanese vessel (believed Japanese Destroyer) 15,000 yds.
SD-4 Air Search Radar
Performance was excellent during the entire patrol. No operational difficulties were encountered. All aircraft were detected at a range of eight miles or greater.
APR-1 & SPL-1
On 5 March 1945 the power transformer in the SPL-1 unit developed a short after approximately 500 hours of operation — second transformer to fail after this period of operation. Operation was restored when the transformer was replaced by a spare. The power transformer of the BN shorted on 25 March 1945. As no spare is carried for this transformer, the BN was inoperative for the remainder of the patrol.

Health, Food & Habitability

Health was generally good. There were several colds the first week after leaving Subic Bay. One man was on the binnacle list for two weeks with a fever from a cold. The meals were well planned and served, except that 354 lbs. of meat spoiled and had to be surveyed, thereby limiting the menu slightly. Being the first boat to refit at Subic Bay, recreational facilities were very limited and the crew lived on board during the refit.

U.S.S. PAMPANITO (SS383)  ///  Report of Sixth War Patrol  ///  Weather / Tidal
Weather

The northeast monsoon was steady in the mouth of the Gulf of Siam, south of Cochin China. Both wind and sea were from the northeast, with wind varying in intensity from about force two to force four. In general, the weather conditions followed closely those as described in coast pilot, with frequent overcast skies, some haziness, and light rain squalls.

From area to Saipan, heavy weather was encountered almost daily with heavy head seas. Skies were usually overcast.

Tidal Information

In the lower Gulf of Siam the set was to the southwest averaging about 0.9 knots, steady during March. Over near the Malay Coast off Great Redang Island the set was southward about 0.5 knots — corresponding with the coast pilot. South of Great Redang, southerly currents increased to about 1–2 knots along the coast.

Density Layers

Bathythermograph cards were taken for each dive made on station. The shallow water in the assigned patrol area limited the deepest dive to 150 feet. In both calm seas and seas of force five, the cards — with one exception — were iso-thermal. The one exception was the dive of 7 March 1945 in 37 fathoms of water and with seas and wind of force one. This card showed an iso-ballast to 60 feet, a negative gradient of 1½° from 60 feet to 80 feet, and an iso-ballast from 80 feet to 120 feet. Surface water temperature varied from 80° to 82° F.; air temperature varied from 77° to 84°.

U.S.S. PAMPANITO (SS383)  ///  Report of Sixth War Patrol  ///  Commanding Officer's Remarks
Remarks

Since Pampanito had spent her last patrol in Area D-2 and had made its only two contacts on enemy convoys well to seaward of the mouth of the Gulf of Siam, and yet knowing that some of the northbound traffic moved well inside the Gulf, it was considered both wise and feasible for the pack to endeavor to maintain an east-west patrol line in this area to cover the most likely routes for shipping both inshore and to seaward. In order to do this thoroughly, surface cruising during daylight was almost a necessity. This method was used during the first part of patrol with no contacts.

Pampanito, having had only a short run from Subic to the area, had ample fuel to continue surface patrolling during daylight the remainder of the patrol, while the other boats were not so well-supplied, having come up from Fremantle. So it was requested that our southern boundary of area be extended from 6° N. to 5° N., in order to give a shorter and more compact east-west patrol line from the Malay Coast eastward and allow part-time submerged patrolling for the other boats. This request was granted and at the same time Caiman was ordered to join the pack.

An east-west staggered patrol line was thus established between Latitudes 5° and 6° N. from the Malay Coast eastward for about 150 miles. The two boats inshore on this line remained submerged during daylight to avoid detection by aircraft while the two boats to seaward maintained surface cruising. It was while using this plan that Sealion sank an unescorted tanker off the coast at night, and Pampanito made its only contact with a high-speed destroyer — though unable to close for an attack. These were the only two contacts made by the Pack prior to leaving the patrol area. While in the area, Pampanito remained on the surface at all times, except when diving for plane contacts and for one day while effecting repairs to the gyro compass, in order to maintain radio guard and coordinate Wolf Pack disposition in case of contacts.

Anti-Submarine Measures & Evasion Tactics

Other than the SJ contact at extreme range on a possible high-speed destroyer the night of 18 March 1945, the only contacts were with enemy planes. These occurred almost daily — both for Pampanito and for the other boats in the pack. Due to intermittent cloudy skies, very seldom were we able to have sight contact prior to diving. At no time did any of these planes close fast, nor did they attempt to drive home an attack on any of us — although we are certain we were sighted more than once. The intermittent cloudy skies and sometimes hazy weather made it expedient to use an intermittent sweep on SD radar during daylight hours (5-second sweep every 2–3 minutes) and it worked fine as far as detecting enemy planes, but Commanding Officer is positive it was also a "Dead give-away" as to location, and that the Japanese kept Pampanito pretty well spotted in both night and day by their detecting equipment.
